I have two years in at an enterprise company, a newborn at home, and I just realized something that sounds obvious but changed how I actually work: **I was chasing the wrong question.**

For months, I'd wake up tired. I'd avoid hard problems. I'd hope someone else on the team would solve it so I wouldn't have to. And every time I did that, I felt this low hum of regret. Not because I failed—because I wasn't becoming who I said I wanted to be.

That's the disconnect nobody talks about.

## The Identity Shift

Here's what happened: I stopped asking "What do I want to achieve?" and started asking "Who do I want to become?"

The difference sounds small. It's not.

When I told myself I wanted to learn and grow and share my work, but then escaped every hard problem at work, those things didn't match. There was friction. Regret. Because my *actions* weren't aligned with my *identity*—who I actually wanted to be.

So I made a decision: every time a hard problem showed up at work—tight deadline, pressure, something I didn't know—I'd volunteer. I'd research it myself. I'd figure it out instead of staying quiet and hoping someone else handled it.

Not because I suddenly wanted to. But because that's what someone who "learns and grows" actually does.

## Commit Imperfectly, Ship Fast

The second piece: **stop waiting to be ready.**

I was overthinking. Worried every video I made had to be perfect, every code commit had to be flawless. That thinking doesn't ship anything. It just paralyzes you.

So I changed it to: commit something trackable every day. A code change. A video posted. *Something* that proves I did the work.

Here's why this matters when you're new to a role: each small commit builds self-trust. Your confidence compounds. You start to *see* yourself as someone who ships, who makes progress, who doesn't wait for perfect. And that becomes real.

The identity forms through action, not intention.

## The Grind Part

I won't lie—this is hard. I started listening to Tony Robbins recordings on my three-day commute to work. Thirty to forty-five minutes each way. In the beginning it felt forced. But after weeks of repetition, the concepts actually stuck. They rewired how I thought about tough days.

That's not motivation magic. That's just grinding through something long enough that it becomes part of how you think.

As a new dad with limited time, I could've used that as an excuse. Instead, I used identity as the anchor: *your future self matters more than your comfort right now.*

## Two Things

1. **Figure out who you want to become.** Not what you want to achieve. Who. Then align your actions around that identity. Every decision—volunteer for hard work or avoid it?—becomes clearer.

2. **Commit often. Ship imperfectly.** Code changes, YouTube videos, Slack messages—something trackable every day. Progress you can see. That builds the self-trust that carries you through the hard parts.

I'm still learning this. But over the past three or four months, the person I am has shifted. Not because I hit some milestone. Because I started acting like who I wanted to become and didn't stop.

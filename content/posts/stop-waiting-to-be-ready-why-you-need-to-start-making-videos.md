I spent the first three years of my career staying quiet in meetings, hoping someone else would explain the architecture or pitch the idea. I could write code all day, but the moment I had to present something—even to my own team—my brain short-circuited.

Then I started recording videos. Not because I wanted to be a YouTuber. Because I realized every single high-leverage moment in my career happens on camera now: interviews, one-on-ones with managers, demos, client calls, async updates to remote teams. And I was terrible at all of it.

## The Real Problem: You're Treating Video Like Content

Here's what I thought making videos meant: perfect lighting, scripted words, no mistakes, production value. So I never started.

What I actually learned: making videos is just repetition at the thing you're worst at. It's public practice.

The first video I recorded, I messed up seventeen times. I had to restart. My voice sounded weird. I edited for six hours. It was genuinely painful. But here's what happened: the second video took three hours. The third one, ninety minutes. By month three, I stopped rehearsing in my head before I hit record. I just... talked.

And that's when the real shift happened.

## Why This Actually Works for Your Career

Every time I've had to present to leadership, explain a technical decision to a non-technical stakeholder, or pitch an idea in a meeting, I'm already comfortable. Because I've done it a hundred times. Alone. On camera. Where the stakes were zero.

But more than that—recording forces you to organize your thoughts. You can't ramble when you're on video. You have to know what you're saying. So you get better at your actual thinking.

I've also noticed I listen better now. I ask clearer questions. I don't negotiate with myself about whether my idea is worth saying—I just say it. That's not confidence from nowhere. That's confidence from reps.

## The Stupid Simple Way to Start

You don't need a ring light or a microphone. Record yourself talking about something you fixed at work today. Pick anything: a bug you solved, a library you learned, a deploy that went wrong. Talk for five minutes. Publish it somewhere—YouTube, a personal site, Discord, anywhere.

The point isn't the audience. The point is you shipped something instead of waiting until it was perfect.

I did this for two weeks before I got comfortable. Two months before it felt normal. Six months in, not doing it felt harder than doing it.

## The Thing Nobody Talks About

Making videos rewires how you see yourself. You stop being "the person who's scared to speak up" and become "the person who makes videos." That identity shift is real and it compounds.

You also realize everyone else who's presenting, pitching, or speaking is doing the exact same thing you are: figuring it out as they go, making mistakes, learning. You just can't see their fear on screen. Once you start, you're suddenly in a group of people you didn't know existed, and the only thing separating you from them was actually pressing record.

Start this week. Not when you're ready. When you're scared—that's when you know it matters.

Most advice about getting better at your job assumes you have time to study. If you're new to a stack, you often don't. What helped me most was a habit so small it felt like cheating: I write my goals down twice a day.

## The two-times-a-day habit

In the morning, I write my goals. In the evening before bed, I write them again. That's the whole system.

The first goal on my list is almost embarrassingly basic: I will get up when the alarm goes off. It's a promise to myself, and it's the one that started everything else. Keeping it is a win every single day, and winning daily is what makes the next goal easier to keep.

The rest of the list is about the chapter I'm in right now. Some of mine:

- Be attentive to my wife and present with my daughter.
- Build technical skills and experience by taking on hard problems where I can practice engineering and problem solving.
- Gain 30 pounds of muscle by sleeping well, eating more, and lifting heavy.
- Stay focused and build good habits, because that helps every other goal.

Your list will look different. The point is that it's specific and it's about who you're becoming now.

## Why it works

I don't finish everything on the list every day. I'm not sure anyone does. Writing the goals still does three things:

1. **It creates small wins.** Hitting one or two a day adds up over weeks.
2. **It stops the leak.** Without a written target, my time drifts to my phone or to whatever's easiest. Writing it down twice a day pulls me back.
3. **It makes good habits easier and bad ones harder.** Every time I choose the good option, I'm reinforcing it. That's the compounding part.

## The feedback loop

This is the part I'd steal first. Every week or so, something breaks. Maybe I communicate badly at home, or I drop a thread at work. I notice it fast, because that's the feedback. Then I turn it into a goal, write it down twice a day, and work on it for a few days.

After a couple of days, I stop needing to remember it, because it's already part of the routine. Then I check whether it's a fit. If it matters, it stays. If it doesn't, I put it aside, but I keep writing the list, because the habit is the thing that's working.

## Applying this when you're catching up

If you got moved onto a stack you didn't choose, the same loop works for learning:

- **Pick one gap per week.** Not "learn Kubernetes." Something like "be able to read and modify our deployment config without asking for help."
- **Write it in both your morning and evening lists.** Repetition keeps it in front of you during the day.
- **Use your gap time.** I listen to books on commutes, while doing chores, and in waiting rooms. Learning can fill those slots too. Pick one idea, test it at work, and see what changes.
- **Review weekly.** Did the gap shrink? If not, change the goal, not the habit.

AI tools can help with the learning step: explaining unfamiliar code, generating practice problems, or summarizing docs you'd otherwise skim. But the loop does the heavy lifting. The list keeps you on the target, and the weekly check tells you whether the target is still the right one.

## The takeaway

You don't need a new productivity system. Write down the two or three things that matter this season, twice a day.
